Books & Personal Libraries

What Australians borrow, what the world reads, and which writers get remembered

By Braeden Som · October 2026

In 2023–24 Australians borrowed almost 174 million items from their public libraries, about six and a half for every person. But what is actually on those shelves? Which kinds of books do readers choose, which do they love, and how have those choices changed? This page follows the story in five parts, from Australia's local libraries to the world's most-read books and the writers critics honour.

Part 1 of 5

Australia's shelves

For many Australians, books start at the local library. But how much we borrow, and how many libraries we have nearby, depends a lot on which state we live in.

On the left map, the darker the brown, the more books each person borrows a year. On the right, the bigger the book symbol, the more library branches a state has. Queenslanders borrow the most, 7.8 items each a year, while people in the Northern Territory borrow the least, 3.7. Yet more libraries do not always mean more borrowing: Western Australia has 233 branches, more than South Australia and Tasmania combined, but borrows only 5.5 items per person.

Source: Australian Public Libraries Statistical Report 2023–24 (National and State Libraries Australasia and ALIA). State boundaries: Australian Bureau of Statistics.

More of what we borrow is digital

Library collections have also changed. Australia's public libraries now hold almost 48 million items, up from 37 million five years ago, and most of that growth is digital (teal in the chart). Ebooks and audiobooks more than tripled, from 4.3 million to 13.6 million, and grew from 12% to 28% of everything on the shelves. Printed items (grey) grew by only about 6%. Borrowing has followed: in Queensland and the ACT, about four in every ten loans are now ebooks or audiobooks.

Source: Australian Public Libraries Statistical Report 2023–24, five-year national comparison.

Part 2 of 5

What's on the shelf

The books Australians borrow and buy are largely the same bestsellers read around the world. To see what fills that shared shelf, this part looks at the 10,000 books with the most ratings on Goodreads, the world's largest book-rating website. Readers there sort books onto "shelves" such as fantasy or romance, and each book here is given the genre readers chose most often.

Source: goodbooks-10k, Goodreads data (Zając, 2017).

Fantasy leads the shelf

Fantasy is the most common genre among the world's most-read books, with 1,914 titles, followed closely by romance (1,728) and non-fiction (1,645). Poetry is the rarest, with just 143. Together, fantasy and romance make up more than a third of the whole list, and adding non-fiction takes it past half. Fantasy books also draw the most readers each: the typical fantasy book has about 26,000 ratings, more than any other genre. At the other end, poetry, graphic novels and horror together make up fewer than one in ten books.

Being common is one thing. Are some genres also better liked?

Some genres are rated more kindly

Each hill shows how a genre's books are spread across star ratings: the higher the hill above a rating, the more books scored there, and the further right it sits, the kinder the ratings. The darker the hill, the higher the genre's typical rating. The dashed line marks 4 stars, and the number beside each hill is the share of that genre's books rated 4 stars or more.

Graphic novels are rated most generously, with 82% of them averaging four stars or more. Horror is the harshest, with only 35%. Even so, ratings across all 10,000 books sit in a narrow band: only 339 books average under 3.5 stars, and just 144 reach 4.5 or more. Even horror classics like Frankenstein (3.75) and Dracula (3.98) land just under four stars.

Source: goodbooks-10k (Goodreads).

Source: goodbooks-10k (Goodreads), 589 million ratings.

Fantasy gets a quarter of all the attention

Each column is a genre. The wider the column, the more ratings that genre receives, and the colours show how those ratings split, from 1 star at the bottom (lightest blue) to 5 stars at the top (darkest blue).

Fantasy is 19% of the books but receives 26.5% of all 589 million ratings, and nearly half of them are five stars. In every genre, only 5–9% of ratings are one or two stars. Fantasy and romance together receive 43% of all ratings. Across the whole site, nearly three in every four ratings are four or five stars, so a good rating is the norm rather than the exception.

These are today's favourites. But have readers always reached for the same kinds of books?

Part 3 of 5

How tastes have changed

Grouping the same 10,000 books by the decade they were first published shows how the mix of favourites has shifted over 70 years.

Each column is one decade and adds up to 100%, so the darker the purple, the bigger that genre's share of the decade's favourites. Books from the 1950s to the 1970s are spread across many genres: non-fiction, children's books, science fiction and historical fiction. Since 2000, two genres have taken over, and in the 2010s romance (28%) and fantasy (22%) make up half of all favourites. One caution: these are books people still read today, so the older decades only include the books that lasted.

Source: goodbooks-10k (Goodreads), books first published 1950–2017.

Australians are searching for romance and fantasy

Is this shift still happening here? Google searches say yes. Australian searches for romance books (rose) roughly doubled between 2019 and 2025, and searches for fantasy books (gold) rose by about 70%, while the other genres (grey) barely moved. These are the same years BookTok, the book community on TikTok, boosted sales of both genres.

Google measures searches on a scale from 0 to 100, where 100 is the busiest month for any of these searches, and each line here is averaged over 12 months to smooth out seasonal spikes. Google changed how it collects data in 2022, but the rise continues well after that change. Click a genre in the legend to follow it on its own.

Fantasy and romance are what readers pick up. But are the most popular books also the best-loved?

Source: Google Trends, Australia, Books & Literature category, 2011–2026.

Part 4 of 5

Popular versus loved

You might expect the books with the most readers to also have the best ratings. They often do not.

Source: goodbooks-10k (Goodreads).

Six in ten books sit between 3.8 and 4.2 stars

Each square groups books with a similar number of ratings (left to right, where each step means ten times more) and a similar star rating (bottom to top). The darker the purple, the more books it holds. Use the menu under the chart to see one genre at a time.

If popular books were better loved, the coloured area would climb towards the top right. It does not. Twilight has 3.9 million ratings but only 3.57 stars, while The Complete Calvin and Hobbes has far fewer readers and 4.82 stars. Sydney-born Markus Zusak's The Book Thief manages both: 1.2 million ratings and 4.36 stars. Overall, the 100 most-rated books average 4.07 stars, barely above the 4.00 average of the other 9,900.

Even favourite authors have misses

Readers rate the book, not the name. Each line runs from an author's lowest-rated book (light blue) to their highest-rated book (dark blue), so a longer line means more uneven books. J.K. Rowling's books range from 3.28 stars for The Casual Vacancy, her first novel for adults, to 4.61 for Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows. Stephen King spans almost as wide a range, while Dan Brown is the most consistent, between 3.60 and 3.85. Even Jane Austen ranges from 3.56 for her early novella Lady Susan to 4.24 for Pride and Prejudice. In fact, 11 of these 12 authors have at least one book below four stars and at least one above it.

Readers decide what is popular. But who decides which writers are remembered?

Source: goodbooks-10k (Goodreads). Box sets and collections excluded.

Part 5 of 5

Who gets remembered

Readers choose bestsellers, but critics choose prize-winners. The best-known literary award is the Nobel Prize in Literature, given to one writer (occasionally two) almost every year since 1901. Where do the writers it honours come from?

Drag the slider under the map to watch the winners build up year by year. The prize has gone overwhelmingly to writers born in Europe. Not one of the 123 winners was born in Australia: Patrick White, Australia's only winner (1973), was born in London to Australian parents.

Source: Nobel Prize API (Nobel Prize Outreach). World map: Natural Earth.

Where winners come from is one side of the story. When in their lives does the prize find them?

Each bar is one winner's life, from birth to death (or to today), and the dark tick marks the year they won. Winners run in prize order from 1901 at the top to 2026 at the bottom, with women in orange. Most winners are honoured late in life: the typical winner was 67, and 24 of them died within five years of their prize. Where readers reward the books of the moment, the prize looks back over whole careers. The orange bars show how slowly the prize opened up to women. Only 5 of the 40 winners before 1946 were women, just one woman won between 1946 and 1989, and since 1990 13 of 37 winners have been women, including Canada's Anne Carson in 2026. Click "Woman" or "Man" in the legend to show one group at a time.

Source: Nobel Prize API (Nobel Prize Outreach), 1901–2026.

So what does all of this mean for Australian readers?

What it means for Australian readers

Australians are borrowing more books than ever, and more of them are digital. The books on our shelves, though, are largely global bestsellers, and since 2000 they have narrowed towards fantasy and romance, a shift Australians' own searches show is still growing. Popularity and quality rarely line up: most books sit around four stars however many people read them, and even the best-loved authors have misses. Meanwhile the writers critics honour are mostly European, usually recognised late in life, only recently women, and just once Australian.

Next time you borrow a book, there is plenty of room on the shelf for something different, and for more Australian voices.
